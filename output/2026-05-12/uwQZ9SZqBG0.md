**1. Stephen A. Smith, on player legacy, coaching**
[00:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uwQZ9SZqBG0&t=19s)

"Let them know, yo, be real, be true, don't be no damn phony and always appreciate cats who are the same. If they real, you don't have to like everything they say, but if they real and authentic, value that. And so I was always, always raised like that. So that literally helped me because when I went to the pro, when I went to covering the pros and stuff, some of my favorites was Derrick Coleman, Rod Strickland, Charles Oakley, and these cats. And it was like, what did they all had in common? No bullshit. You know what I'm saying? Don't come up to them with no phony shit. It ain't gonna work. You know what I'm saying? So it was like, yeah, when when they sit up there and they rocked with me and they was cool with me, I like, yeah, I'm doing something right."

_Why it matters:_ Stephen A. Smith explains how his upbringing, which emphasized authenticity, shaped his interactions with NBA players like Derrick Coleman, Rod Strickland, and Charles Oakley, earning their respect by being genuine.

**3. Stephen A. Smith, on player legacy, coaching**
[01:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uwQZ9SZqBG0&t=91s)

"I was a shooter. I was a shooter. I cracked my kneecap in half, never had much of a college career. High school, I was too small. I was playing, you know, but I grew up when Rod Strickland, Dwayne 'Pearl' Washington, cats in New York like Boo Harvey who had gone to St. John's and cats like that, you know, Mark Jackson, who was at, you know, he went to St. John's as well. I knew my lane, you know, so it was always like that, but those cats again, being around them helped me because there was a level of realness that they required. And so because of that, I was like, okay, you know, I ain't good enough to play, but I'm damn sure good enough to write and report and talk about this shit and and and provide a perspective that they couldn't provide because it wasn't YouTube back then. It wasn't podcasting back then."

_Why it matters:_ Stephen A. Smith reflects on his own basketball career, acknowledging he wasn't at the level of legendary streetballers like Rod Strickland and Mark Jackson, but credits their 'realness' for inspiring his pivot to sports journalism.

**4. Stephen A. Smith, on front office, team dynamics**
[02:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uwQZ9SZqBG0&t=144s)

"So much had to be dependent on the reporters. My whole thing was, if you talk to me, I'm at your mercy because it's your story to tell. But if you don't talk to me, then you're leaving it up to me to interpret it in any way I want to. Based on what I see, what I hear, and what I know, I'm going to come up to my own conclusion. Ultimately, that led to me being able to cultivate an inordinate amount of relationships over the years to build my career."

_Why it matters:_ Stephen A. Smith explains his approach to sourcing information, emphasizing that his conclusions are drawn independently if sources don't engage, which is crucial for understanding how major NBA stories are often shaped and reported.

**5. Stephen A. Smith, on player legacy, coaching**
[03:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uwQZ9SZqBG0&t=227s)

"I was at Winston-Salem State. First year there, first couple of months... I cracked my kneecap in half, never had much of a college career. My mother's insurance, because they don't have those upgraded facilities at these Division two colleges that they might have elsewhere, certainly not in the pros. So in order to really rehab, I had to go under my mother's medical insurance to pay for it. And they wouldn't do that in North Carolina. So they made me have to go back to New York to rehab. I had to skip school for a semester, go home, rehab my knee before I came back, earn my basketball scholarship back, that kind of thing. And when that happened, my mother sat down and she was like, 'Well, there goes your basketball dreams. That's over.' She said, 'Not that you had big time dreams in that regard anyway, but it's definitely over now because you're probably not going to be the same. So what you gonna do with the rest of your life?'"

_Why it matters:_ Stephen A. Smith recounts how a severe knee injury in college effectively ended his basketball aspirations, leading his mother to challenge him about his future and setting the stage for his career pivot.

**6. Stephen A. Smith, on coaching, player legacy**
[05:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uwQZ9SZqBG0&t=325s)

"And I told her, 'I'm gonna be on television.' Exactly what I said to you. Be on television, right? And I'm like, it just came out of my mouth. And I'm like, okay, well, my sister looked at me, my older sister Linda, because I got four older sisters, and she was like, 'That sounds nice, but what the hell you gonna do? Smile for the camera?' And she said, 'That ain't you. You smile when people make you laugh. You smile when some shit is funny, but you ain't gonna just smile and be smiling. That ain't you. And what kind of career is that supposed to have you reading a prompt or whatever, whatever.' She was like, 'Nah, you gotta have more substance than that.' And I said, 'So I write newspapers first.' And then I started writing for the school newspaper. I was a disc jockey on the school radio station. I started writing for the weekly newspaper, and then I got a job at the daily news, at the local daily newspaper, and then I interned at the Atlanta Journal Constitution, came back and interned at the Greensboro News and Record, and then I got the job at the New York Daily News as a high school writer, and my career took off from there."

_Why it matters:_ Stephen A. Smith reveals his spontaneous decision to pursue a television career after his basketball dreams ended, spurred by his sister's challenge to find a path with more 'substance' than just smiling for the camera, leading him through various newspaper roles.
